4 October 2026 · Battery manufacturing

Price Wars: East vs. West Battery Production and the SynBatt Response

Why this mattersCheaper, reliable battery production helps clean energy scale. Knowing which costs engineering can change makes the next investment more useful.

A gigafactory announcement is a promise. A cell delivered to a customer is a different thing. The map tracks six years of battery production: the largest Chinese producers expand their global deployment, European startups announce ambitious plants, and some companies fail before those plans become repeatable production.

The video omits the dataset's North American entries: EnerDel, American Battery Factory (ABF), Our Next Energy (ONE), Factorial, Amprius, and PowerCo's St Thomas project. They remain in the sourced dataset, alongside the off-map Agratas project in Sanand, India.

This is a selected-company view, not a complete list of factories or failures. It also resists a tempting “Asia wins, the West disappears” narrative. Japanese and Korean makers do not all follow the same growth curve, while European factories that are ramping up deserve to remain visible alongside the failures. The useful question is what separates a promising project from a plant that can keep shipping good cells at a competitive cost.

What went wrong and what each company is changing

The black dots do not have one shared cause. An unfinished funding round, an unstable production ramp, and an expensive factory atmosphere need different responses. The cases below distinguish documented bottlenecks from possible engineering responses.

Northvolt's production ramp outlasted its funding runway. Its March 2025 bankruptcy statement identifies internal ramp-up difficulties alongside higher capital costs, supply-chain disruption, and changing demand. Its September 2024 response had already been to concentrate on cell manufacturing at Northvolt Ett, its gigafactory in Skellefteå, Sweden, put upstream cathode production into care and maintenance, and reduce the scope of other activities. [23, 24]

The failure mechanism is a feedback loop: delayed reliable output postpones customer income, while the plant and its expansion continue consuming cash. Ramp-up requires enough time and funding to stabilise and qualify the process before further expansion. Better yield, uptime, and process control address that bottleneck; a cheaper dry room alone would not establish that Northvolt could have survived.

At Britishvolt, grant milestones required spending before the business had enough cash to reach them. EY's administrators describe a business with no material revenue and a large financing shortfall. Management planned £800 million of equity in 2022; the report records £167.5 million raised across its equity rounds. The £100 million government grant required spending milestones that were unlikely to be reached without substantial additional investment first. Administration followed on 17 January 2023. [25]

This was principally a financing bridge problem before mass production, rather than evidence that a working European line could not match China's coating cost. A smaller, staged factory and financing aligned with achievable milestones might reduce that exposure. That is a proposed lesson, not a demonstrated alternative outcome.

For PowerCo, the factory atmosphere was part of the production system to be installed before the equipment could run. A 2025 update quoting its Spanish CFO describes 17 clean rooms covering 57,000 m² at Valencia, with some also requiring dry conditions, and the installation of those rooms before production equipment. Salzgitter was commissioned in December 2025 with initial nameplate capacity of 20 GWh/year. Neither announcement is a measurement of saleable annual output. [26, 27]

The physical pain point is straightforward: a large conditioned volume means more infrastructure to install and an air-handling system to operate before the first good cell earns revenue. PowerCo's own 2026 Bundestag disclosure names its participation in MiKoBatt, which investigates factory and machine concepts using micro-environments. Its project research targets the volume of air being conditioned. That is a documented response to the infrastructure lever. [28, 19]

The regulatory detail deserves equal precision. Germany's Gefahrstoffverordnung §10 restricts returning extracted air from relevant hazardous work areas, while permitting an exception for adequately cleaned air using recognised procedures or equipment. Positive pressure is a way to protect a product against incoming contamination; it is not permission to push hazardous vapour into occupied space. A viable machine enclosure must solve product protection, worker exposure, and ventilation together. [29]

An air-recirculating, pressurised machine enclosure could reduce conditioning loads; its suitability depends on containment, air cleaning, and exposure evidence. That comparison has to be made between designs handling the same substances. The engineering opportunity is to shrink the volume that needs stringent conditioning while maintaining both worker safety and electrode quality, rather than simply copy another factory's pressure setting.

Morrow inaugurated its 1 GWh/year Arendal LFP factory in August 2024. Its announcement described test production and further equipment tuning, not full-capacity commercial output. It also reported 366,000 working hours to install the dry room, utilities, and production equipment together. A low-carbon location still requires a substantial manufacturing system. [35]

One unusually concrete bottleneck appears in Morrow's own October 2024 LinkedIn post: it said an immigration-rule interpretation prevented South Korean subcontractor specialists from travelling to install equipment, threatening deliveries with up to four months of delay. This is the company's account of the dispute. The cost mechanism is clear: specialised commissioning knowledge must reach the factory before installed equipment becomes productive. [36]

On 6 May 2026, Morrow announced its decision to file for bankruptcy. Its board identified oversupply and price pressure, higher capital costs, industrialisation delays, and a restrained investment market; an industrial-investor process could not finish before liquidity ran out, despite recent supply agreements. The response had been to seek industrial backing and financing. It arrived too late for the company in its existing form. [37]

The perspectives differ usefully. Process engineer Miguel Pereira's May 2026 LinkedIn account stresses manufacturing progress and the need for patient investors; Intercalation Station's June postmortem questions LFP profitability, next-generation chemistry readiness, and dependence on imported materials. These are attributed engineering and editorial interpretations, not audited explanations of the bankruptcy. Together they expose the tension: process progress, competitive product economics, and sufficient runway must coincide. [38, 39]

FREYR sanctioned a 29 GWh/year Giga Arctic plant in June 2022. By November 2023, its own results reported that commissioning the qualification plant's casting and cell-assembly equipment had slipped beyond the fourth quarter. It increased vendor coordination and technical support, cut spending, and minimised Giga Arctic investment while seeking more competitive policy conditions. The company explicitly compared European support with US and Canadian incentives. [40, 41]

FREYR had to commission an unfamiliar production system while financing scale-up in a location with less favourable incentives. In November 2024, FREYR announced a pivot into US solar manufacturing and termination of its 24M technology licence. Its amber diamond records that pivot rather than a bankruptcy. The engineering lesson is to validate the critical machines before extrapolating a promised cost advantage to a gigafactory. [42]

AMTE Power had a proposed funding agreement, but no bridge funds to keep trading while it completed. In December 2023, the subscription completion date was pushed back by due diligence and the holiday period, and its proposed investor declined to advance requested bridge funds. The board said it had insufficient money to continue trading. A January 2024 announcement confirmed administrators had been appointed. [43, 44]

FRP subsequently reported selling the Thurso manufacturing business and assets to LionVolt, preserving twenty jobs. The company failed; manufacturing assets could still have a future. Here the documented immediate trigger is the timing of rescue funding, rather than a demonstrated dry-room or chemistry defect. [45]

ACC was learning to manufacture while customers' product requirements were changing. Its 2024 report describes first-factory process learning and a slower European EV market. In June 2024, its CEO explained pausing German and Italian factory development while investigating a less expensive product offering; the French factory continued ramping up. [11, 30]

By February 2026 the problem had gone beyond a temporary pause: the UILM union said ACC management had confirmed shelving the Termoli and German projects. That is a union account of a management meeting, while the mapped French project remains distinct. [46]

The trap is committing to one chemistry, customer segment, and expansion schedule while the market moves towards a different price point. The response combines process stabilisation with a revised product and investment roadmap. Shelving expansion is different from an ACC bankruptcy. A fast line making the wrong-priced product is not automatically a competitive line.

LG Energy Solution's January 2026 results attributed lower 2025 revenue to slower EV sales at major customers. Its fourth-quarter operating loss was KRW 122 billion including KRW 332.8 billion in North American production incentives. Subtracting that incentive gives a loss of KRW 454.8 billion before the incentive. This is an accounting comparison, not a cell-cost estimate. [47]

Its announced 2026 response is practical: redirect idle EV capacity towards storage, expand lower-cost LFP and mid-nickel products, and reduce CAPEX by more than 40% relative to 2025. It also plans an LFP dry-electrode pilot. These are company plans, not outcomes assumed in this article. Even an experienced Korean producer has to solve utilisation, product mix, and incentive exposure; “East versus West” is a starting point, not a complete explanation. [47]

VARTA's CoinPower range consists of rechargeable lithium-ion cells in a round, flat button-cell format, with wound electrodes. Its technical handbook describes winding the anode, cathode, and separator into a coil inside a stainless-steel housing. One concrete format is the CP1254 A4X: 12.1 mm diameter × 5.4 mm height, 3.7 V nominal voltage, and 74 mAh nominal capacity. These are miniature cells, rather than the automotive cells behind the map's EV-deployment measurements. [57, 58]

VARTA's November 2022 statement identifies several pressures acting together: rising energy and raw-material prices, customer production interruptions and semiconductor shortages, and weaker demand for true wireless stereo headsets using CoinPower. It says input-cost increases could be passed on only partly and with a delay. The response included short-time working at Nördlingen, production-capacity adjustments, and cost reductions. [59]

The 2024 annual report shows a recovery: €101.1 million revenue, €64.2 million personnel expenses, and €14.9 million adjusted EBITDA in the Lithium-Ion Small Cells segment, which represents CoinPower. Its adjusted EBITDA margin recovered from −25.0% in 2023 to 14.7% in 2024. VARTA attributes the improvement to higher call-offs from a major customer and more flexible production structures. EBITDA is measured before depreciation, interest, and taxes; these figures do not establish an individual cell's manufacturing cost or its cost gap against a Chinese competitor. [60, p. 44]

By this article's cutoff, the company faced a further customer shock. VARTA's 2 October 2026 update says insolvency proceedings opened on 1 October for VARTA AG and three subsidiaries. It separately states that the planned Nördlingen closure followed the loss of its most important customer. VARTA Consumer Batteries is unaffected; a black VARTA AG marker does not mean every business bearing the brand has stopped. [61]

My reading is that this case is about the interaction of a continuing cost base, fluctuating utilisation, limited price pass-through, and customer concentration. An established line can make good cells and still lose its economic foundation when orders disappear. The cited public material supports those pressures; it does not quantify a CoinPower unit-cost disadvantage or establish a lithium-export tariff as the cause.

Verkor secured financing and a customer link before ramping up. Its 2023 announcement ties the initial 16 GWh/year Dunkirk project to funding and a Renault partnership. In August 2026 it reported commissioning a second line and delivering the first batteries produced entirely on-site. [13, 31]

The remaining challenge is to turn those milestones into qualified, repeatable output and good utilisation. Financing and a customer relationship address two risks; they do not disclose achieved annual yield or prove cost parity. I keep the announced capacity outlined on the map for exactly that reason.

EnerDel's 2023 Chapter 7 filing is recorded on the timeline, but the available evidence used here does not establish a specific process-cost cause. ABF's startup and Tucson factory announcements are plans, not documented bankruptcy or measured deployment. Attributing either case to NMP regulation or an expensive dry room would go beyond that evidence. [32, 33]

The cross-case lesson is that engineering savings have to arrive before the company exhausts its cash, and the resulting cells must have a customer. The twenty mechanisms below explain why one country's headline price cannot diagnose every company's failure.

Why not fund it until it works?

If Europe needs battery factories, why would an automaker stop supporting one after investing hundreds of millions? First, separate equity from purchase commitments. Volkswagen announced an additional €500 million investment in Northvolt in June 2021. BMW participated in Northvolt's 2019 equity round, but those announcements do not establish a €500 million BMW investment. BMW's widely reported €2 billion commitment was a battery-cell supply contract, with deliveries originally planned from 2024, rather than €2 billion already paid into the company. [48, 49, 50]

In June 2024 BMW confirmed cancelling that order. Its statement to Reuters still expressed interest in European sustainable cell manufacturing and described a joint focus on next-generation cells. Cancellation of one contract therefore should not be read as a documented decision to abandon electrification or every relationship with Northvolt. BMW had other suppliers, and had already announced CATL and EVE contracts for its sixth-generation round cells in 2022. [51, 52]

The next euro has a different job from the last euro. Previous investment may have built valuable equipment, knowledge, and customer relationships. Those assets belong in a forward-looking rescue assessment. But money already spent does not, by itself, establish that another round will produce usable cells in time. My interpretation is that a rational continuation decision has to answer three questions:

  • Can the technical bottleneck be closed? Is there measured progress in yield, throughput, and customer qualification, with a credible timetable for the remaining work?
  • Can the cash bridge cover the whole ramp? Will funding last through commissioning and qualification, rather than merely postpone the same liquidity crisis?
  • Does the resulting product still fit the buyer? A vehicle launch cannot wait indefinitely, and a cell designed for one generation may not serve the next architecture.

Northvolt's creditors and customers did try another bridge. Its November 2024 restructuring announcement identified access to approximately $145 million in cash collateral and a customer's $100 million commitment in new financing. The Swedish bankruptcy followed in March 2025. That sequence demonstrates additional willingness to fund operations; it does not establish whether a larger coordinated rescue would have succeeded. [53, 23]

A private buyer and Europe do not value the same benefits. BMW can pursue its EV programme using other cell suppliers. Europe also has an interest in local skills, supplier diversity, and industrial resilience. That creates a possible coordination problem: the benefits of keeping a factory alive spread across customers and society, while the next investor bears a concentrated risk. Patient capital can be justified by those future benefits, but it should be tied to an independently assessed technical recovery, committed customers, and enough runway to finish it. “Always rescue” and “withdraw at the first delay” are both poor project strategies.

The policy argument also needs precision. EU legislation introduced a 100% fleet CO₂ reduction target for new cars and vans from 2035; it does not ban driving existing combustion cars. The Commission has proposed replacing that target with 90% plus compensation mechanisms, a revision still discussed by the Council in June 2026. A proposal is distinct from enacted law. Either framework creates a strong electrification incentive, but neither guarantees demand for a particular European supplier's cells. [54, 55]

Finally, ending the original company is different from abandoning its industrial assets. Lyten announced completion of its acquisition of Northvolt Ett, Ett Expansion, and Northvolt Labs in February 2026, with a planned commercial restart. The map keeps Northvolt's company failure black and adds the acquired Ett project under Lyten separately. Continuation can happen through new ownership; the restart still has to prove its economics. [56]

First, compare the same thing

BloombergNEF's 2025 survey puts the global average lithium-ion pack selling price at $108/kWh, China's average at $84/kWh, and Europe's average 56% above China's. That implies roughly $131/kWh in Europe. These are market averages, not identical-cell factory cost estimates. Its stationary-storage pack average is $70/kWh; LFP and NMC packs average $81 and $128/kWh respectively. Product mix changes the comparison substantially. [1]

A quote for a bare Chinese stationary-storage cell cannot be compared directly with a European automotive pack. Packaging, thermal management, electronics, qualification, and the application differ. Nor does a 56% premium mean Europe must cut its own price by 56%: moving from $131 to $84 is a reduction of about 36%. That arithmetic still describes a large challenge, but it gives the right denominator.

The IEA's 2026 review confirms China's dominant manufacturing position and the continuing shift towards LFP. Yet regional selling prices include different supply origins and product mixes. I would treat the headline price gap as a signal to investigate, not a measurement of the savings available from changing one process. [2]

Two compounding effects: currency and trade policy

A declining dollar selling price and a weaker dollar are separate pressures. In an illustrative, unhedged example, a $100 order brings €90.91 at $1.10 per euro, but €83.33 at $1.20 per euro. If the dollar price also falls to $90, receipts become €75.00, a 17.5% reduction from the starting point, while euro-denominated factory costs may remain. The exchange rates and order values in this example are hypothetical.

This does not affect every producer equally. Dollar-denominated inputs, local costs, currency hedges, and contract terms determine net exposure. VARTA's 2024 report specifically says procurement and sales currency effects largely offset across the group, with different effects in individual segments. That is not evidence that CoinPower suffered the full gross-revenue effect in the example. [60, pp. 56, 157]

Twenty reasons the gap persists, in numbers

Each factor compares a European reference or conventional route with a cheaper or more efficient benchmark. The unit matters: parts, staff, time, and power are not interchangeable with total cell cost. Where factory purchasing data are private, the comparison uses a published model or an explicitly labelled scenario. These are the available benchmarks, not proof of the cheapest possible factory.

The product and its supply chain

  1. Chemistry mix: roughly 1.67× or more in pack price. The IEA reports LFP packs were over 40% cheaper than NMC in 2025: NMC/LFP > 1/0.60. EU EV demand remained predominantly nickel-based. Storage applications affect the average; this is not a matched-cell experiment. Market comparison · [2]
  2. Specification: 1.24× the pack mass for the same energy. The IEA's 2025 examples reach 205 Wh/kg for LFP and 255 Wh/kg for nickel-based packs: 255/205 = 1.24. Choosing cheaper chemistry changes mass and packaging requirements. This physical ratio is not a manufacturing-cost penalty. Product benchmark · [67, p. 123]
  3. Electrode processing: 1.10× the cell cost. A 2026 LFP study models an optimised dry route 9.3% below its water-based reference: 1/0.907 = 1.10. The corresponding cell costs are approximately €45.1 versus €49.8/kWh. This is a process scenario, not an EU–China measurement. Cost model · [5]
  4. Pack architecture: 1.67× as many parts. CATL claimed its first cell-to-pack design removed 40% of conventional pack parts: 1/0.60 = 1.67. That reduces assembly work, but does not establish an equal cost saving or describe every current European pack. Manufacturer claim · [68]
  5. Lithium purchasing: 1.27× the input price. The IEA assumes approximately €61.9/kg of lithium content for EU buyers versus €48.7/kg for major Chinese buyers. The inputs reflect bargaining power and integration, not a uniform tariff; they are not prices per kilogram of lithium carbonate. Cost-model inputs · [67, Annex A]
  6. Materials sourcing: 1.24× input price. Modelled discount: 19.5%, calibrated to reported Chinese prices; purchasing contracts are undisclosed. Model assumption · [4, §3.1.2]
  7. Manufacturing experience: roughly 10× the cumulative volume in China. By 2024, China had cumulatively produced about ten times Europe's EV battery volume. More output provides more opportunities to learn; ten times the volume does not imply ten times lower costs. Historical production · [67, p. 119]
  8. Late rejection: 5× scrap. Modelling 5% versus 1% end-of-line rejects lowers cell cost 4.2%. Research scenario · [4, §3.3.3]

The plant that actually runs

  1. Equipment: about 2× machine price. Chinese-supplier equipment is assumed to cost half the European benchmark. Model inputs · [4, §3.1.2]
  2. Scrap and downtime: 3× the inefficiency allowance. The IEA assumes 15% for the EU and 5% for China, combining rejects and downtime. For equal input and other conditions, 95%/85% implies about 1.12× the cost per good unit, rather than 3× total cost. Model assumption · [67, Annex A]
  3. Capacity utilisation: 1.70× the fixed cost in a half-used plant. At 50% versus 85% utilisation, fixed cost per kWh scales as 85/50 = 1.70, holding yield constant. The IEA uses 85% as a planning ceiling; 50% here is an illustration, not a measured European average or Chinese benchmark. Illustrative calculation · [69]
  4. Factory planning: 1.80× time. German model: 4.5 years; Chinese expert estimate: 2.5 years before production. Model / estimate · [4, §3.1]
  5. Staffing and automation: 3.57× the labour intensity. The IEA assumes 125 workers/GWh for the EU versus 35 in China: 125/35 = 3.57. This measures staffing for equivalent output, before differences in hourly pay. Cost-model inputs · [67, Annex A]
  6. Specialist pay: 5.86× hourly wage. German/Chinese inputs: €45.4/€7.7 per hour. Model inputs · [4, Table 1]

Buildings, energy, and the operating schedule

  1. Construction: 1.33× cost/m². German/Chinese inputs: €3,129/€2,347; factory areas vary independently. Model inputs · [4, Table 1]
  2. Electricity: 2.46× tariff. German/Chinese inputs: €0.183/€0.074 per kWh, before energy-demand differences. Model inputs · [4, Table 1]
  3. Gas: 1.23× tariff. German/Chinese inputs: €0.051/€0.042 per kWh. Model inputs · [4, Table 1]
  4. Dry-room scheduling: 4.59× the power in the active setting. Buck's model reduces power by 78.23% in a lowered, unoccupied operating mode: 1/0.2177 = 4.59. This is an idle-period opportunity, not evidence that occupied production can run at the same saving or that MiKoBatt has already achieved it. Operating-mode scenario · [6]

The cash needed to reach competitive production

  1. Ramp-up: 1.99× spending. Doubled duration raises modelled German ramp-up cost from €170 million to €339 million. Delay scenario · [4, §3.2]
  2. Output support: 1.57× breakeven price without aid. Modelled credit: €31/kWh; German levelized cost: €85.8 versus €54.8/kWh. Policy scenario · [4, §3.3.1]

These factors cannot be multiplied together: staffing, scrap, throughput, and utilisation overlap, while several comparisons change the product itself. Subsidies change what a producer must recover from sales, rather than making the physical process cheaper. The output credit above is a modelling experiment based on a historical US incentive, not a claim about current EU eligibility.

Monetary model inputs are shown in euros using the ECB's 2025 annual average of 1.12998 US dollars per euro, without an inflation adjustment; factors use the unrounded original inputs. [70] Supplier clusters, qualification, financing terms, customer commitments, and margin compression also matter, but the public sources do not provide comparable company-level cost factors for each.

A denominator worth watching

Good kWh = nominal capacity × utilisation × yield.

As a deliberately simplified illustration, 50% utilisation and 80% yield deliver 40% of nominal capacity. At 85% utilisation and 95% yield, the same nameplate delivers about 81%. An unchanged annual fixed-cost bill is then spread over roughly twice the good output. These are illustrative assumptions, not measurements of any company on the map.

The dry room is a lever, not the whole explanation

Two requirements are easy to conflate. Solvent control protects people from hazardous exposure during processes such as NMP-based coating. Moisture control protects sensitive materials and cell assembly. They can interact in a factory design, but they are not the same problem.

NMP presents reproductive toxicity hazards. ECHA's guidance addresses exposure control under REACH; Germany's TRGS 402 describes assessing inhalation exposure. GefStoffV §10's recirculation constraint and its cleaning exception apply to relevant hazardous work areas. They do not prescribe a universal whole-factory dry-room volume or dew point. Enclosure, extraction, air handling, and required dryness depend on the process and the exposure assessment. [14, 15, 29]

It is also worth checking what an infrastructure cost estimate describes. The often-cited €120.7 million CAPEX and €18.1 million annual OPEX figures in the 2025 EDAG coverage refer to an improved factory-layout scenario, not a universal baseline dry-room price; the OPEX is not labelled exclusively as energy expenditure. I would not use those numbers as proof that one room explains a regional price premium. [16]

The productive question is more specific: what air volume and moisture load does this material, machine, transfer, and maintenance procedure really require? Local enclosure can reduce the volume being conditioned. It still has to cope with leakage, product entry, people, interventions, and safe extraction. Relaxing a dew-point requirement is a result to validate against cell quality, not a saving to assume before testing.

What the 2026 literature is trying to change

These six publications make the discussion concrete. Five are journal papers; the last is a policy modelling brief. They describe different parts of the problem, and their modelled results should not be read as savings already demonstrated across gigafactories.

  1. April 2026 · Factory economicsBridging the global cost gap in battery cell manufacturing

    Lechner and colleagues connect location, factory planning, ramp-up, and subsidy design. The implication is to optimise plant performance and policy together, rather than choose a country from wages alone. The reported outcomes depend on the modelled factory and assumptions. Paper [4] ↗

  2. January 2026 issue · Dry LFP electrodesCosts and carbon footprint analysis of dry manufacturing battery LiFePO4-based cathodes

    Greitemeier and colleagues extend CellEst with laboratory and industry data. Their optimised dry-electrode scenario reduces total cell cost by 9.3% against a water-based reference. This is a chemistry- and scenario-specific estimate, not a claim that dry coating erases the regional pack-price gap. Paper [5] ↗

  3. March 2026 · Factory atmosphereModel based evaluation of dry rooms in battery production

    Buck and colleagues model dry-room loads and air handling. This provides a route to sizing infrastructure around the real operating conditions. It does not make every battery process compatible with an ordinary hall. Paper [6] ↗

  4. June 2026 · Technology selectionA multi-criteria decision analysis approach for evaluating production technology in battery cell manufacturing

    Wicke, Neef, and Tübke compare technology through cost, throughput, quality, and sustainability criteria. The useful contribution is an explicit decision framework; no one process wins independently of the application and assumptions. Paper [7] ↗

  5. January 2026 issue · Dry-process maturityInsights into dry battery electrode manufacturing: Unveiling the patent landscape

    Greitemeier and Lux map the competing dry-process approaches and their patent activity. This helps distinguish a broad technology label from specific routes and ownership. Patents document development activity, not production yield or an achieved cost advantage. Paper [17] ↗

  6. March 2026 · Scale and policyThe falling cost gap between EU and Chinese batteries

    Transport & Environment explores how learning, scale, and policy could narrow the gap. It is an advocacy organisation's scenario analysis, with assumed improvements in scrap and automation, rather than a measurement of factories that have already converged. Brief [8] ↗

SynBatt: change the process, then prove the economics

The BMFTR-supported SynBatt transfer cluster connects research and industrial implementation in battery cell production. Its projects attack several of the manufacturing levers above. I see their value in making better production routes testable: on realistic materials, at useful throughput, with measured quality and a complete cost balance. [18]

The BMFTR-funded MiKoBatt project, with PowerCo as an industrial partner, investigates micro-environments around machinery and material handling. The idea is to condition a smaller volume near the product instead of assuming that every surrounding cubic metre needs the same specification. Its 2026 project poster also makes the hard parts visible: transfers, interventions, and integration with the equipment. A smaller enclosure is useful only if it preserves product quality and safe operation. [19, 28]

SkaleD focuses on bringing direct extrusion coating towards industrial production, including double-sided electrodes. Using less solvent can reduce the drying burden, but high-viscosity material must still be fed, distributed, and coated uniformly. The objective is a stable process window and a competitive whole line, with less energy spent removing solvent. [20]

E-MoPEd develops simultaneous, automated double-sided multilayer coating and inline process control. It targets productive throughput and resource efficiency together. The trade-off is familiar: the machine must increase useful output without increasing coating defects or creating a downstream bottleneck. Throughput gains have to survive the quality check. [21]

GranuGoIn is the project I work on personally. It runs from January 2025 to December 2027 and develops granulate-based semi-dry electrode production, including extrusion, calendering, dosing, and inline quality control. Its public description is more specific than “remove every solvent”: the objective is to reduce solvent and drying requirements and compare the resulting route with conventional wet processing. [22]

That distinction matters. Fully dry binder-fibrillation routes are another important research direction, but they are not interchangeable with this project's semi-dry description. Nor does solvent reduction remove the need to control dust, moisture, or other production hazards. The engineering question remains whether granule preparation and processing deliver repeatable electrodes, good cells, and a lower cost per good kWh.

For my work on machine learning, this makes process stability a practical target. A model that connects changing material properties and process settings to electrode quality can help us find reliable operating conditions. Faster predictions are useful if they support decisions that improve the physical process; a convincing model alone does not establish a manufacturing saving.

The target is a factory that keeps making good cells

Europe cannot purchase competitiveness by announcing more nameplate capacity. Nor should it assume that conventional wet coating is financially dead. Mature wet routes still compete, and each alternative has to earn its place through yield, performance, throughput, safety, and lifetime economics.

Micro-environments, reduced-solvent coating, multilayer processing, and semi-dry granulation offer credible ways to change the production system. They belong alongside better utilisation, materials sourcing, financing, and customer demand. SynBatt addresses part of that system; it is not a demonstrated recipe for an $80/kWh pack.

I want the next European dot on this map to grow because it ships reliable cells, not because its planned capacity was drawn larger. That is a measurable challenge and a better starting point for research than treating an entire price gap as the cost of a dry room.

Company markers use approximate headquarters or the named project site, with leader lines to offset labels. Brief halos highlight new milestones without encoding output, and dates beside project names are planned start years. Established makers' deployment is global; it is not output at that dot's geographical location. The choice of companies is illustrative and incomplete. A marker's first appearance can be the first selected announcement, rather than the company's founding date. VARTA AG enters with its 2022 cost-and-demand statement; no EV-deployment volume is assigned to its miniature CoinPower cells. Its black marker dates the opening of proceedings on 1 October 2026 and excludes Consumer Batteries. A failed company can have assets acquired or restarted under another owner; its black dot does not assert that a factory will remain closed forever. Project phases follow the milestones included here, not a live status feed.

Historical deployment values use SNE's following-year comparison column where available, because reporting coverage is revised. CATL's 2020 value comes from its corporate brochure; the other 2020 observations are left unavailable. Amber diamonds distinguish funding distress, project spending pauses, and strategy pivots; grey outlined targets retain the paused plan, rather than represent output. The Morrow marker uses its board's filing announcement; AMTE uses its regulatory confirmation of administrator appointments. Sources and reporting windows accompany the table.
