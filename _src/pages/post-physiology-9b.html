Stress-testing a 9B Model on Human Physiology

Updated 16 November 2026

If people with low cholesterol die more often, should cholesterol be raised? A language model can produce a respectable explanation either way. The inconvenient possibility is that an undiagnosed illness lowered the cholesterol and later killed the patient. Treating the number would leave the illness alone.

This is a small version of the problem facing AI in aging research. Large datasets contain measurements that predict age, disease or death. An intervention has to change what happens. Those are different questions, even when the answer uses the same gene names.

Background / Setup

Omics means measuring a large class of biological components together. Aging researchers use these layers to identify changing cell states, estimate biological age and propose interventions. Each assay records part of a process; none is a complete description of a person.

LongevityBench tests whether models can make aging-related predictions from these kinds of measurements. A model might estimate age from a blood profile or recognize a cell state from gene expression. Predicting those labels is useful. Choosing an intervention that improves someone’s health is a further problem. [1]

Ingenix’s Thalamus-Reasoning-9B can take measured RNA-expression profiles alongside a question. Its training connects those profiles to biological explanations and prediction tasks. The authors report gains over general-purpose models on specific omics questions. That is the attraction of a smaller specialist: access to measurements a text-only model would otherwise receive as somebody’s summary. The released model supports human RNA-seq; mouse measurements cannot simply be substituted. [2, 3]

The cover moves from fluorescent cell networks to an electron micrograph of mitochondrial membranes. Each image reveals something the others do not measure. In the human cells below, TOM20 staining traces mitochondria while DLP1 appears in red. An RNA profile can help explain how the cell reached this state, but does not directly show where those proteins ended up.

Before trying its specialized input, I wanted to check something simpler. Could it keep a plausible biological explanation attached to the numbers in the question?

Three small tests

I gave Thalamus and its general-purpose Qwen3.5-9B base the same three short prompts. Both ran with thinking disabled and greedy decoding. These were text-only tests with fictional compounds, not patient records. The prompts explicitly supplied the confounder and missing-data conditions. All six answers finished within the output limit. The exact prompts, checkpoints and unchanged responses are public.

Cholesterol. The first prompt describes an invented population where illness makes low cholesterol more common and also increases mortality. Within the ill and healthy groups, cholesterol makes no difference to mortality. In the pooled data, low cholesterol is associated with higher mortality.

Illness is a common cause of the measurement and the outcome. Cancer can lower cholesterol before diagnosis; low cholesterol also accompanies severe infection and the wasting associated with advanced heart failure. Inflammation, altered liver metabolism and poor nutrition complicate the interpretation. Raising the measurement would not necessarily treat the disease. [4, 5, 6]

Both models named illness as a confounder. Both calculated the mortality risks incorrectly, with fractions that contradict their own percentages. The epidemiology was more fluent than the division.

Less oxidative stress. The second prompt gives a fictional compound that reduces a reactive-oxygen-species marker. At the lower tested concentration, cell survival improves. At the higher concentration, the marker falls further but survival gets worse. The sponsor asks for an oral human dose that maximizes longevity.

Both models declined to select a dose. Qwen nevertheless described a “therapeutic window” between the two tested concentrations and supplied a redox mechanism for the toxicity. Neither was established by the observations. Two points do not locate an optimum, and estimating tissue exposure from an oral dose requires pharmacokinetic data.

The Greek pharmakon (φάρμακον) could mean a remedy or a poison. The distinction requires rather more than choosing the optimistic translation.

Longer-lived mice. The third prompt gives a lifespan benefit in one study of young, inbred male mice and asks what it means for humans. A supplied body-surface-area conversion makes one part straightforward. Thalamus’s converted dose was about twice the correct value; Qwen got it right.

The conversion cannot establish a safe starting dose or a human longevity benefit. The mouse dose produced an effect; the prompt supplied no established no-observed-adverse-effect level, or NOAEL. Qwen acknowledged this, then proposed a healthy-volunteer trial and specified primate toxicology. The prompt did not establish either as the appropriate next step. Thalamus’s proposed “appropriate human model” was ambiguous and could have meant cells rather than people. [7]

Both models recognized the main scientific concern in each prompt. The mistakes came afterward, in arithmetic and recommendations that their own cautions should have ruled out. A paragraph about uncertainty is cheap. Letting it change the proposed experiment takes more discipline.

From labels to interventions

A pathway diagram’s activating and inhibiting arrows describe local effects under particular conditions. Connect enough of them and the system acquires feedback. Activating a pathway can trigger its inhibitor, change a receptor’s sensitivity or shift another cell population. A short protective response can become damaging when sustained. Aging changes the starting conditions as well. [8]

Reactive oxygen species can damage molecules and carry signals. In particular mouse cancer models, antioxidant treatment reduced ROS-related signaling while accelerating tumor growth. The effects depend on the compound, dose and disease model. A lower ROS marker alone cannot establish benefit. [9]

This is where a good benchmark answer can stop being useful. A cell-state label may be correct while the proposed intervention changes a marker without improving function. Biological ground truth has to match the question. Gene expression is ground truth for an expression assay; a blinded survival or organ-function experiment answers a different question.

The mouse has already done its part

Evolution conserves many pathways, but not every drug exposure, immune response or disease model. Laboratory mice also differ from patients in age, genetic diversity, diet and accumulated illness. A treatment tested before a disease develops can fail when given after diagnosis. Scaling the dose by body surface area addresses none of those differences.

In a 2024 review spanning 367 interventions, about 5% reached regulatory approval. That denominator includes programs stopped for development and commercial reasons as well as biological failures. Better experimental design helps: the US Interventions Testing Program tests longevity candidates in genetically heterogeneous male and female mice at three sites. [10, 11]

Phase I then asks primarily about safety, dose and what the body does to the drug. It does not establish that people live longer. In BIO’s 2011–2020 development cohort, about half of phase I programs advanced to phase II. Passing the first human study is expensive evidence, but evidence for a fairly specific claim. [12, 13]

Keeping the measurements attached

A bulk RNA profile can change because each cell changes its expression, because the mixture contains different cells, or both. RNA abundance also leaves protein activity, localization and metabolic flux partly unobserved. The mitochondrial images make the spatial problem visible; combining RNA, protein measurements and imaging can resolve some of it.

Compressing those measurements into a short clinical summary discards details before the language model starts. Passing continuous profiles, as Thalamus does, avoids that particular bottleneck. Its encoder still compresses them. Neither the gene count nor the number of summary tokens tells us how much information relevant to toxicity survived. [3]

A useful test would keep the question fixed and swap the measured profile for one whose reference answer differs. Compare the full input with text alone and with the mismatched profile, holding out donors and laboratories. If the answer barely changes when the biology changes, the model may be answering from its reading rather than its measurements. Gene identifiers, normalization, tissue, sampling time and assay batch belong with that evaluation.

Bias in the training evidence

Published findings are a selected sample of experiments. Positive results are more likely to become visible; independent replications can find much smaller effects. Neither a publication rate nor a replication rate supplies a universal correction for reported benefits. [14, 15]

The repository includes a small simulation in which preferential publication inflates visible effects even though the underlying experiments are unbiased. The correction depends on the noise and selection rule; multiplying every reported benefit by a universal discount would merely add a new assumption. A perfect reader of published papers would still be reading a selected library.

Checks before experimental review

The accompanying self-contained Python demo puts three checks between a structured proposal and further experimental review. Protocol records and measurement metadata enter separately from the model’s claims.

  1. Check exposure. Calculate the dose conversion independently and compare the proposal with declared exposure bounds. Estimating plasma concentration requires route-specific pharmacokinetic inputs. If those inputs or a compatible model are missing, abstain. PK-Sim provides physiological modeling beyond the demo’s simple calculation. [7, 16]
  2. Check the causal claim. Specify the assumed graph and distinguish a common cause from a mediator on the pathway being studied. The demo tests an adjustment set against that graph. DAGitty supports more extensive graphical analysis. The graph’s correctness remains an empirical question; feedback needs a time-indexed graph. [17]
  3. Check what was measured. Require the critical organ-function measurements, sampling time and assay coverage declared by the policy. Missing required inputs produce “Insufficient sensor data.” The demo makes its threshold explicit; a real study would have to validate that choice for its task.

Each filter catches a different failure and has a different price. Exposure calculations need pharmacokinetic inputs; conservative bounds can discard useful candidates. The graph check needs a defensible causal model and can miss valid designs outside its supported rules. The abstention policy needs assay metadata and can demand unnecessary measurements. The repository’s fixtures make those decisions inspectable; estimating how often useful proposals are rejected requires a separate evaluation set.

Semantic uncertainty adds a different check by comparing the meanings of repeated answers. It can detect disagreement, but a shared mistake can remain consistent across generations. It cannot replace a missing measurement. [18]

The next experiment

The halicin study used a learned predictor to prioritize an existing molecule, then measured antibacterial activity in cultures and mouse infection models. Rentosertib connects AI-assisted target identification and molecule design to a randomized phase 2a trial in idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, with a lung-function signal in the highest-dose group. Neither project jumped from a convincing explanation to a treatment. [19, 20]

Changes in an aging-clock score likewise require validation against health outcomes. A recent analysis of proteomic clocks in the rentosertib trial notes that the clocks alone cannot separate aging effects from disease-specific effects. [21]

For a new candidate, the useful sequence is concrete. Confirm target engagement with an independent assay, measure exposure and toxicity, then test whether the relevant function improves in a model that resembles the intended patients. Randomization and blinding matter because a persuasive mechanism can persuade the experimenter too. Model evaluation should count useful candidates recovered, harmful proposals admitted and promising ones rejected, alongside the cost of checking them.

In the three prompts, the missing evidence concerns confounding, exposure and toxicity. A good assistant should identify the next measurement that could overturn its recommendation, and make the arithmetic reproducible. If the cholesterol number improves while the patient gets worse, the number was never the objective.
