6 September 2026 · LLMs in science

I checked 2,294 arXiv equations for upside-down fractions

If you copy a rendered formula out of a chat window and paste it into another chat, the second model doesn't necessarily get the TeX. It gets whatever text the browser pulled out of the page, and for a fraction that text can come out upside down. The second model then rebuilds the formula from a flat string, and nothing in that string says which part went on top.

Most of what we know about LLMs in papers comes from word counts in abstracts and paragraphs. By early 2024, up to 17.5% of computer science abstracts showed signs of LLM editing,2 at least 13.5% of 2024 biomedical abstracts did,3 and by the end of 2025 most biomedical papers had some.4 None of that says anything about the math. So I wanted to know how often a flipped fraction ends up in the equations themselves, and pulled the LaTeX source of 200 arXiv papers to go through them.

The short version: about one display equation in twenty has an error that changes what it says. Inverted fractions are a very small part of that. The common errors are much more boring, and their rate hasn't moved since 2021. What has moved is the typography of the source files.

Where the flip comes from

KaTeX, which ChatGPT and several other chat interfaces use to render math, writes every formula into the page twice. One copy is MathML for screen readers, with the original TeX tucked into an annotation. The other is the visible layer of positioned boxes, and that layer stacks a fraction from the bottom up. In the text of the page, the denominator comes before the numerator.

This is what the text of each layer contains for three formulas, rendered with KaTeX 0.16.11 (the version this site uses):

FormulaMathML layerVisible layer
\(P = \frac{TP}{TP+FP}\)P=TPTP+FPP=TP+FPTP
\(\mathrm{softmax}\big(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\big)\)softmax(QK⊤dk)softmax(dkQK⊤)
\(k = A\exp\big(-\frac{E_a}{RT}\big)\)k=Aexp(−EaRT)k=Aexp(−RTEa)

Which of these lands on the clipboard depends on the browser, the app, and whether the site ships KaTeX's copy-tex extension, which exists because a plain copy gives you the math twice.1 If the second model only sees the visible layer, reading P=TP+FPTP as TP+FP over TP is a perfectly reasonable guess. For precision a human would notice. For a formula nobody knows by heart, the flipped version compiles, looks fine, and goes into the paper.

What I sampled

Four arXiv categories: machine learning (cs.LG), computer vision (cs.CV), materials science (cond-mat.mtrl-sci) and signal processing (eess.SP). Two months per year for 2021, 2023, 2024, 2025 and 2026, with 2021 as the baseline from before ChatGPT. For each category and month I jumped to a random page of the monthly listing and took the first five papers with a LaTeX source under 25 MB. That gives 200 papers, 40 per year. One turned out to be a 2018 paper that showed up in a listing, so it sits out the year comparison.

The 200 sources contain 6,591 display equation rows (every line of an align counts on its own), 44,903 pieces of inline math, 5,130 \frac and 1,694 slashes. 26 papers have no display equation at all.

I did two things with them.

First, pattern checks on all 200 papers. These looked for slashes followed by a product (a/bc, where the reader has to guess how far the denominator reaches), textbook formulas with a known orientation (attention scaling, precision and recall, IoU, cosine similarity, Boltzmann factors, z-scores, Adam, Bayes, Coulomb and a few more), and symbols the same paper defines once as a/b and once as b/a.

Second, a line-by-line review of every display equation in 60 papers, three per category and year. The reviewer was Claude, working from a fixed protocol. For every row it had to decide whether a careful reader using the equation exactly as printed would get something other than what the authors mean, and if so name the error, the corrected form, and the evidence from the paper. Each of the 189 flagged errors then went through a separate pass whose job was to reject it: 179 held up, 3 were reclassified and 7 were dropped. I checked the inversions and a handful of others by hand.

To get a feel for what the reviewer misses, a second independent review of four papers (120 rows) found 15 rows with meaning-changing errors, 14 of them the same rows the first review had flagged. Two runs of the same model tend to miss the same things, so treat that as a ceiling on recall, not a measurement.

Errors come in two grades. Changes the meaning is a wrong sign, a missing factor, a wrong index: use the equation as printed and you compute something else. Cosmetic is visibly wrong but can only be read one way, like an extra closing parenthesis. The numbers below are for meaning-changing errors unless I say otherwise.

How many equations are wrong

112 of 2,294 reviewed display rows have at least one meaning-changing error. That's 4.9%, with a 95% interval of 3.5 to 6.4% from resampling papers. Counting cosmetic errors too, it's 7.6%.

The average hides how uneven this is. Half the papers, 30 of 60, have no meaning-changing error in any display equation. The other half spreads out, and a few papers carry a lot: 11 have more than 10% of their display rows wrong. In one paper the only display equation was wrong, and another got 5 of its 9 wrong.

The inversions I did find

The review found two, the pattern check turned up a third outside the review sample, and there was one more in words.

A 2021 signal-processing paper writes a matrix as \(H^{-1}K_1H^{-1}\) where its own derivation needs \(HK_1H\). That one predates ChatGPT, which is a useful reminder that people flipped things long before.

A 2025 federated learning paper has an envelope-theorem step:

$$-\frac{dI}{dL_{\text{task}}} = \frac{\partial_{\lambda}\, L_{\text{task}}}{\partial_{\lambda}\, I}$$

The chain rule gives the right-hand side the other way up. The same section also swaps sup and inf twice, turns a min-max into a max-min, and flips two inequalities. Of all 60 reviewed papers, this source looks the most like pasted chat output. It has 49 Unicode hyphens (U+2010), which nobody types in a LaTeX editor, and inline math written as \( \) in 121 places next to ordinary dollar signs.

A 2026 materials paper weights vacancy configurations by \(p_i \propto \exp(\epsilon_i/k_BT)\), with \(\epsilon_i\) the formation energy. The next sentences explain that the configuration with the lowest formation energy takes essentially all vacancies, which needs \(\exp(-\epsilon_i/k_BT)\). The Boltzmann weight is printed as its reciprocal.

And in words: a 2024 unlearning paper defines its pruning score as forget importance over retain importance, then describes it in the appendix as "the ratio of the retain importance to the forget importance."

The two ambiguous ones are a 2026 paper on stress rates that writes \(\cosh^{-1}(2\alpha)\) for \(1/\cosh(2\alpha)\), which any reader takes for arcosh, and a 2025 condensed-matter paper where \(M_e\omega/2\hbar\) is only right if you read it left to right, against the paper's own habit of writing \(\hbar^2k^2/2M\) for \(\hbar^2k^2/(2M)\).

Only the 2025 federated learning paper has anything in its source that looks like chat output. The rest read like ordinary slips.

Ambiguous slashes

The pattern check over all 200 papers flagged 110 slashes, and I read each one in context. Most weren't a precedence question at all: units like mJ/cm², derivatives written dV/dλ, sample labels like BN/WSe₂/BN. 14 papers use the physics habit of writing ħ²k²/2m or Ω/2π for ħ²k²/(2m) and Ω/(2π). Physicists all read those the same way, so I don't count them.

Four papers, one in machine learning and three in signal processing, have a slash whose reach a careful reader could get wrong. One example is \(\Theta(l^2C_n\sqrt{\log l}/\alpha\eta_e\sqrt{C_p})\), where it's anyone's guess how much of the tail belongs in the denominator. Three of the four are from 2026.

The textbook-orientation check matched 33 formulas (cosine similarity, precision, attention scaling and so on), and all 33 were the right way up. No paper defined a symbol as a/b in one place and b/a in another.

Put together, the probability that a paper has an ambiguous or inverted fraction is 4 in 60 reviewed papers, 6.7% (2.6 to 15.9%), for meaning-changing errors in display equations. The pattern check over all 200, which also covers inline math but only recognises specific shapes, finds 5 papers, or 2.5%.

The error rate doesn't trend. The year-to-year swings mostly come down to which bin two or three long papers happen to land in.

What does trend is the source code. I counted three things that rarely come from typing LaTeX by hand: Unicode em dashes, the Unicode and non-breaking hyphens that chat interfaces emit, and inline math written as \( \) more than five times. In 2021, 6 of 39 papers had any of them. In 2025 it was 17 of 40, and in 2026 15 of 40. The last three columns count papers out of all 40 per year, not just the reviewed ones.

The usual LLM word list (delve, intricate, showcasing, underscores and so on) peaks in 2024 at a median of 3.6 per 10,000 words and drops back to 1.5 in 2026. That fits people learning to strip the obvious words, while the pasted hyphens and dashes stay in.

Among the reviewed papers, the 15 with a chat fingerprint have a pooled error rate of 7.7% against 4.2% for the rest, but a lower average per paper (6.1% against 7.2%). With 15 papers that's no signal in either direction. The rank correlation between a paper's LLM-vocabulary rate and its error rate is −0.03.

Caveats

60 reviewed papers and two inversions make for wide intervals. A true rate three times higher would not surprise me.

The reviewer is a language model. The rejection pass and the second review catch false alarms and give some sense of what gets missed, but neither is a human referee.

Only display equations were reviewed. Inline math, where a lot of quick a/b lives, only went through the pattern check.

This is the arXiv LaTeX source, not the journal version. Errors fixed in proofs still count here, and errors introduced by a publisher don't.

Four categories isn't all of science. Theory-heavy fields would look different, probably with more equations per paper and more places to go wrong.

Nothing here tells you why an equation is wrong. A flipped ratio in a paper full of pasted hyphens is suggestive, not proof. I'm also not linking the papers, since the point is the rate and not the authors.

The copy-paste flip is real in the sense that the page really does store fractions bottom-up. But across 2,294 equations, it shows up at most once in a paper that looks pasted, and the other inversions are the ordinary kind. The errors worth worrying about are the dull ones: a factor that quietly drops out between the definition and the proof, or an index that points at the wrong time step. They were there at about the same rate in 2021. Whatever LLMs are doing to how papers get written, they haven't visibly changed how often the math is wrong.
