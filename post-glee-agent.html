Measuring the pot, not the play

Notes from building an agent for the GLEE economic-games benchmark in a weekend. There is a playable version of the three games if you want to try them first.

For 48 hours at the end of August I ran a bot in GLEE, a benchmark that drops an agent into three economic games (bargaining over a shrinking pot, negotiating over an item worth more to the buyer than it costs the seller, and a repeated persuasion game with one-sided information) against a live field of other bots and language models. The score is a percentile: you are ranked by where your payoff lands against the rest of the field on that exact configuration, not by how much you win. Efficiency and fairness never enter it. A move is worth whatever the field does in the same spot.

No language model in the agent

A rating with a roughly 100-game memory needs volume to hold a percentile, and a 50 ms move plays far more games than a 3 second one. So the agent is game theory with a small bandit on top: Rubinstein's alternating-offer split, an ANAC-style time concession, the Kamenica–Gentzkow signalling rate, each with a few constants the bandit tunes. That ran about 10,000 offline self-play games a minute, and 16,000 arena games with zero invalid moves.

Normalise the reward by the size of the prize

The bandit's reward was the rank of each payoff within its own history for a context. A context bucket spanned pot sizes from $100 to a million dollars, so the rank recorded which configuration the agent drew, not how well it played, and the arms never separated. Dividing the reward by the prize on offer tripled the gap between a good arm and a bad one (+0.040 to +0.119).

Compare in the same seat, not across the table

The obvious way to check the agent is to compare it to the opponent it just played; by that measure it won by +0.093. But the rating ranks you against others in your seat on your configuration, so the opponent across the table is the wrong reference. It measures the seat, not the play. Against a field agent in the same seat the agent scored 51%, matching the live percentile exactly, which is what made the rest of the diagnosis trustworthy.

The offline field is softer than the live one

I built a population of synthetic opponents to sweep changes against. One change scored 51% to 64% offline; live, over the 148 games before and 176 after, it went from the 50th percentile to the 46th. The synthetic opponents conceded 0.20 to 0.60 of the gap per round; the live median was 0.02, ten to thirty times slower. After that an offline sweep was a hypothesis to test live, not a result.

Inflation was the whole bargaining problem

Once every agent logged each game in full, bargaining had one dominant leak. Against a patient opponent at discount 0.8, the agent took 0.159 of the pot where a simpler variant took 0.373. Its acceptance bar discounted the continuation by a single round, as if the next offer would close, when a real deal took several, and at that discount each extra round costs 20 to 30% of the pot. Discounting by the number of rounds a deal actually takes fixed it: the simpler bot closed 80% of those games in the first round, the original 39%.

What did not move the metric

The levers I was most confident in did the least. Persuasive message styles (structure, concrete reasons, loss framing) barely moved a buyer reading the bare categorical offer. Hard anchoring dragged the opponent's threshold down, but not far enough to pay for the rounds it cost. And an opponent's talk about how patient it was only ever revealed weakness: fitting its discount from its demands let a greedy bluff read as very patient and collapsed the agent's own share to 0.09, so the discount is only inferred downward, never up.

The agent finished around rank 14. The parts that moved the percentile were the dull ones: scale the reward, measure in the right seat, close before inflation eats the pot. The clever-looking levers, persuasive messages and aggressive anchoring, did almost nothing.

What the top of the leaderboard probably did is not more of this; it is about timing. A percentile on a moving field rewards two things I never really played: knowing when to stop, and finding the tactic that pays before the field catches up. Stonewalling in bargaining is the clearest case. While most agents still conceded, an agent that simply refused to move harvested a run of good percentiles off the ones that folded; once everyone stonewalled, the same move only produced deadlocks and no deals, and the edge was gone. The winning play is to adopt a tactic while it is mispriced, ride it, and then freeze the agent to lock in the peak before the counter-adaptation arrives. The strong teams stopped at the top. I kept playing a field that had already moved.

The three games are playable here if you want to feel the seats yourself.
