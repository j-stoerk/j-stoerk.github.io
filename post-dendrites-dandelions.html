Lithium Dendrites Are Batteries' Dandelions

Updated 11 November 2026

On Ansgar Lowack, An Analytical Model of Critical and Subcritical Alkali Metal Dendrite Growth in Ceramic Solid Electrolytes (arXiv:2603.20113⁠).

0:00 / 4:28
AI

Rigid ceramic electrolytes can still be penetrated by metal-filled cracks. Lowack's analytical preprint models lithium and sodium deposition into pre-existing interfacial defects, balancing electrical dissipation against creep and crack opening. Its numerical example uses sodium and Na5SmSi4O12, so the quantitative example below is a sodium-system calculation rather than a lithium prediction. [1⁠]

Pressure, creep and fracture

Depositing metal inside a confined defect adds volume. Soft alkali metal can accommodate some of it by creep; when confinement prevents that relief, pressure can drive the ceramic crack forward. The model combines this fracture cost with the electrical advantage of depositing at an existing conductive tip. It assumes a thin, metal-filled defect with an idealised geometry and material properties. [1⁠]

A root widening a pavement crack offers a limited visual analogy. Both involve growth in an existing confined space and the resistance of the surrounding material. Roots also respond to moisture, oxygen and biology, and tree-root pavement observations do not establish a dendrite law or a dandelion-specific weakest-link distribution. [2⁠]

The electrical and mechanical balance

Onsager's variational framework constrains a coupled dissipative system. It does not say that all current follows a single path of least resistance: current distributes across available paths. In this model, the current field, deposition and mechanical dissipation are treated together. The break-even between the electrical benefit of tip deposition and the cost of crack opening gives the critical-current estimate. [1⁠]

Jcrit≈Y σVMKIcFcmax3/2.J_\mathrm{crit}\approx Y\,\frac{\sigma V_M K_{Ic}}{F c_\mathrm{max}^{3/2}}.Jcrit​≈YFcmax3/2​σVM​KIc​​.

Here σ is ionic conductivity, VM the alkali-metal molar volume, KIc fracture toughness, F the Faraday constant and Y a geometry factor. The expression applies when the longest defect cmax is sufficiently thin to constrain creep. Under those assumptions, doubling its length lowers the estimated threshold by a factor 23/2⁠. A distribution of independent defect lengths can then produce a Weibull-like distribution of critical currents. [1⁠]

Growth below the critical current

Residual electronic conduction can permit recombination and deposition at a defect tip below the critical-fracture threshold. The paper's stress-corrosion model gives

Jsubcrit≈YσVMKIsccFcmax3/2,KIscc=Ef2πaVM.J_\mathrm{subcrit}\approx\frac{Y\sigma V_M K_{Iscc}}{F c_\mathrm{max}^{3/2}},\qquad K_{Iscc}=\frac{E_f\sqrt{2\pi a}}{V_M}.Jsubcrit​≈Fcmax3/2​YσVM​KIscc​​,KIscc​=VM​Ef​2πa​​.

The formation-energy term Ef and electron-jump distance a determine this lower threshold. The electron transference number te does not appear in it. Instead, te enters the estimated failure time, approximately τfail = Fw²/(teVMJc0) under the paper's additional geometric approximations. Reducing electron leakage lengthens that time and suppresses this pathway as te approaches zero. [1⁠]

Subcritical growth matters only if the lower threshold lies below the operating current. Lowack's initial numerical estimates put the two thresholds on a similar scale, while smaller grain-boundary formation energies could open a wider slow-growth regime. Preventing that slow failure would help where a critical-current-only picture might otherwise predict stability, not where it already predicts catastrophic fracture. [1⁠]

What the numerical discrepancy leaves open

Using defects around 30 µm long and 50 nm wide with the sodium-electrolyte constants gives roughly 50 mA/cm²⁠, against a measured scale around 1 mA/cm²⁠. That factor-of-50 mismatch prevents treating the expression as a calibrated design limit. The paper discusses local toughness and grain-boundary effects as possible missing physics; those explanations still require independent measurements. [1⁠]

The sodium example draws on Anton and colleagues' tape-cast Na5RSi4O12 work and related measurements cited by Lowack. It should not be attributed to a general reference on Weibull statistics. [3⁠]

The model suggests tests that can distinguish mechanisms: measure defect geometry, creep and local fracture resistance together; vary electronic leakage while keeping the mechanical interface comparable; and record failure times as well as a stepped-current threshold. Its useful contribution is a set of coupled dependencies and testable assumptions, with the absolute current scale still unresolved.
